AprilTags are designed to be found; a game piece is not. Nobody printed a convenient black-and-white code on it, it can
be anywhere in the image, half-hidden, badly lit, next to three others. For that problem, hand-written rules collapse
(that was [lesson one's](/blog/ai-basics) whole point), so we learn the rules instead. This lesson explains how a neural
network goes from a grid of pixels to a list of boxes, each with a label and a score — and what that looks like running
on our hardware.

## 1. Classification, detection, segmentation

Three vision tasks, sorted by how much they tell you — and how much they cost:

- **Classification**: one label for the whole image. "This photo contains a note." Where? No idea.
- **Detection**: an axis-aligned bounding box around each object, with a label and a confidence score. "Note here
(94%), note there (81%)." This is the task that matters for a robot, because a box in the image is something you can
aim at.
- **Segmentation**: a label for every single pixel — the exact outline. Beautiful, expensive, and overkill for finding
a game piece.

## 2. The convolution: a filter that learns

How do you even feed an image to a network? Naively, you'd wire every pixel to every neuron — millions of weights for a
tiny image, and a network that has to re-learn "what an edge looks like" separately for every location. Terrible idea.

The fix is the **convolution**: a small grid of weights (say $3 \times 3$) that *slides* across the image, computing a
weighted sum at each position. The output is a new image highlighting wherever the pattern appears — an edge, a corner,
a blob of orange. One filter, applied everywhere. In a convolutional network (CNN), the filter weights are learned like
any other parameter, and layers stack: early filters find edges, middle layers find textures and parts, deep layers find
whole objects. Nobody designs that hierarchy; it falls out of training.

Notice what this buys us: the same filter applies at every position, so an object is recognized wherever it sits in the
frame. Remember the *factors of variation* that killed hand-written rules? Convolutions handle the biggest one —
position — for free, by construction. That one idea is most of why deep learning conquered vision.

## 3. How a detector produces boxes

Classification says "there's a note somewhere". How do you get *boxes* out of a network? The family to know is
**YOLO** — *You Only Look Once* — whose name is the whole idea: instead of scanning the image region by region, one
single network pass predicts everything at once.

Conceptually: the image is divided into a grid, and each cell predicts a few candidate boxes — position, size, a
confidence score, and class probabilities. One forward pass, hundreds of candidates, real-time speed. The raw output is
messy (the same object gets found by several neighboring cells), so a cleanup step called **non-maximum suppression**
(NMS) keeps the highest-scoring box and deletes overlapping duplicates.

The specific architectures evolve every year — versions, forks, anchor-free variants, transformer hybrids — and chasing
them is a hobby in itself. The grid-of-predictions picture has survived a decade of that churn, so that's the part worth
storing in your head.

## 4. Measuring a detector

A detector that "seems to work" in the shop is worth nothing until you can put a number on it. The vocabulary:

**IoU** (Intersection over Union) measures how well a predicted box matches the true one: the area of their overlap
divided by the area of their union, from 0 (missed) to 1 (perfect). A prediction usually counts as correct above 0.5.

$$
\text{IoU} = \frac{\text{area}(A \cap B)}{\text{area}(A \cup B)}
$$

**Precision** asks: of the boxes I predicted, how many were real? **Recall** asks: of the real objects, how many did I
find? They fight each other — lower your confidence threshold and recall rises while precision falls. **mAP** (mean
average precision) sweeps the threshold across the whole range and condenses the trade-off into one number, which is why
every detector paper leads with it.

And the golden rule from [lesson one](/blog/ai-basics) applies with full force: all of this is measured on images the
network never trained on. A detector scored on its own training photos is just an expensive way to lie to yourself.

## 5. The data

Here's the part no tutorial dwells on: the model is the easy 10%. The real work is the dataset.

Labeling means a human draws the boxes — hundreds of images, by hand, ideally from real competition footage. Tools like
Roboflow make the drawing tolerable and the exporting painless. A few hundred well-chosen images can be enough for one
game piece; more variety beats more volume.

Then **augmentation** stretches what you have: random rotations, brightness shifts, blur, crops — each labeled image
becomes ten slightly different ones, teaching the network the factors of variation you couldn't photograph.

The classic trap deserves its own sentence: if every photo was taken in your shop, under your shop's lights, you have
trained a shop-detector. It will meet arena lighting for the first time during a match, and it will lose. Photograph
where you'll play, or augment until it hurts.

## 6. On a Limelight or a coprocessor

A Limelight can run two kinds of neural pipelines: a **Neural Detector** (boxes around game pieces — everything this
lesson described) and a **Neural Classifier** (a label for the whole frame). Inference runs on an accelerator rather
than the main processor, and Limelight ships pretrained models for the season's game pieces, plus a documented path for
training and uploading your own.

One caution before you fall in love: the output is a box *in the image* — an angle to the object and an apparent size —
not a field position. Getting from a box to "the note is 2.3 m away at 15°" is a geometry problem, not a learning
problem, and it's a genuinely hard one. Know exactly why you want a detector before spending build-season time on it.
