A large language model (LLM) does exactly one thing: given some text, predict what comes next. That's it. That's the
whole trick. Everything else (answering questions, writing code, translating, holding a conversation) falls out of that
one task, pushed to a scale nobody had seriously tried before. I find this one of the most surprising facts in all of
computing, and by the end of this lesson I hope you see why it's surprising, rather than just accepting it.

## 1. Predicting the next token

Text is first chopped into **tokens**, which are pieces of words, roughly. "Robotics" might be one token, "localization"
two or three. Common words get their own token, rare words get assembled from fragments. The model reads a sequence of
tokens and outputs one thing: a probability for every token in its vocabulary to come next. Sample one, append it, feed
the longer text back in, repeat. Every answer you've ever gotten from a chatbot was produced one token at a time, each
chosen from a probability table.

Sit with what that means. The model has no plan for the paragraph, no database of facts it consults, no notion of true
and false. It continues text in the most plausible way, where "plausible" was learned from a colossal amount of human
writing. The astonishing part, the part nobody fully predicted, is how much apparent understanding falls out of doing
that extremely well. To guess the next token of a physics answer, it helps to have absorbed a lot of actual physics.

---

## 2. Embeddings

For a network, tokens have to be numbers. Each token becomes a vector with thousands of dimensions, called its
**embedding**, and these vectors are learned so that similarity in meaning becomes closeness in space. "Motor" and
"actuator" end up near each other. "Motor" and "poetry" end up far apart.

The classic party trick shows that directions carry meaning too: take the vector for *king*, subtract *man*, add
*woman*, and the nearest vector is *queen*. Meaning, turned into geometry. (How do you measure "near" between vectors?
The dot product, the same one from the [math toolkit](/blog/cheatsheet-math). Told you it was the operation of the
course.)

## 3. Attention and the transformer

Here's the problem embeddings alone don't solve. In "the note the robot dropped", what does "dropped" attach to? Meaning
depends on context, and context means other words, sometimes far away in the sentence.

**Attention** is the mechanism. For each token, the model computes how relevant every other token is, and blends their
information in, weighted by that relevance. "Dropped" attends strongly to "robot" and to "note", and weakly to "the".
Each token's representation gets progressively enriched by exactly the context that matters for it, and the weights
that decide what matters are all learned.

The **transformer** is the architecture built on this: a block of attention plus a small neural network, stacked dozens
of times. Its superpower is less subtle than people expect. Attention across the whole text can be computed massively in
parallel on GPUs. The architecture won partly because it *trains fast*, and at this scale, training speed is destiny.

For the full picture with animations, the 3Blue1Brown videos linked below are unbeatable. I'd genuinely rather you watch
them than read three more of my paragraphs.

## 4. How fifteen pages flipped the tech world

The transformer comes from a single paper: "Attention Is All You Need", published in 2017 by eight researchers at
Google. It's about fifteen pages long, and it was written to solve a fairly narrow problem, machine translation. Nobody
in it claims to be building a general-purpose intelligence. The interesting part is what happened next.

Because a transformer trains so well in parallel, you can feed it far more text than any earlier architecture, and
researchers started doing exactly that. Within a year, OpenAI published the first GPT (*Generative Pre-trained
Transformer*) and Google published BERT, both built on the paper's block. Then came the scaling. GPT-2, in 2019, had 1.5
billion parameters. GPT-3, in 2020, had 175 billion, and it was the first one that could do tasks it had never been
trained for, just from a description in the prompt. ChatGPT, in late 2022, was a fine-tuned descendant of GPT-3 (the
fine-tuning is described in the next section) wrapped in a chat window. It reached a hundred million users in about two
months.

What I find remarkable is that nothing fundamental changed between the 2017 paper and the chatbot on your phone. Same
block, same next-token objective. What changed is the amount of data, the amount of compute, and the willingness to
find out what happens when you push both by a factor of a thousand. In other words, a large part of the modern AI boom
is one good idea, scaled.

## 5. Pre-training, fine-tuning, reasoning

Getting from "next-token predictor" to "assistant" takes three stages, and the distinction explains a lot of behavior
you've seen:

1. **Pre-training**: predict the next token on a huge slice of the internet, books, and code. This is where the
knowledge and the language come from. The result is powerful and completely unhelpful. Ask it a question and it might
continue with three more questions, because that's plausible text too.
2. **Fine-tuning**: train further on examples of good question-answer behavior, so that "helpful assistant" becomes the
plausible continuation.
3. **RLHF** (reinforcement learning from human feedback): humans compare pairs of answers, a reward model learns their
preferences, and the model is optimized against it. That's [reinforcement learning](/blog/ai-basics), the AlphaZero
family of ideas, aimed at politeness and helpfulness instead of chess.

Recent **reasoning models** add a twist that sounds almost too simple: let the model generate a long private chain of
thought (just text) before committing to an answer. More tokens spent "thinking" measurably improves math, code and
logic. It's still next-token prediction. It never stopped being next-token prediction.

## 6. What they don't do

The same mechanism that makes LLMs impressive produces their failures, and you should be able to predict them now:

- **Hallucination.** The model produces plausible text, and false statements can be perfectly plausible. It will cite a
WPILib method that doesn't exist, with a convincing name and signature, in a confident tone. Ask yourself: what in
"predict the next token" would stop it?
- **No self-awareness of ignorance.** Estimating "do I actually know this?" is not part of the training objective.
Confidence and correctness are uncorrelated in the failure cases, which is exactly when you need them correlated.
- **It only sees what you paste.** No access to your robot, your repo, or this year's game manual unless you provide
them. Its knowledge also has a cutoff date. Ask about the newest WPILib release and it may describe last year's.
- **Finite context.** Very long conversations fall off the edge. Details from the start silently stop influencing the
output.

None of these are reasons not to use LLMs. They're reasons to use them with your eyes open.

## 7. Using them for robot code

Where this becomes practical for us. My ground rules, learned the usual way:

- **Give context.** WPILib version, vendor libraries, the actual code excerpt, the error message. "Why doesn't my
swerve work" gets you a horoscope. A stack trace gets you help.
- **Prefer explanations over paste-ready blocks.** "Explain what this pose estimator call does" teaches you something
and survives the model being wrong. Blindly pasted code fails at the worst possible time, which in FRC is called
"quals match 3".
- **Everything gets tested on the robot.** An LLM's confidence is not evidence. The simulator and the practice field
are.
- **Never paste secrets.** Tokens, keys, credentials. Once sent, gone.
- **You own what you commit.** "The AI wrote it" is not a code review, and it won't debug it at competition either.

Used like that, as a very fast, very well-read assistant with no accountability, an LLM is genuinely one of the best
learning tools ever built for a programming student. Used as an oracle, it's a way to ship bugs you don't understand.
