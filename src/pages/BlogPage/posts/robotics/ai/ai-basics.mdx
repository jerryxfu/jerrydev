Here's a paradox worth sitting with. The first computers were excellent at what humans find hard — calculating,
sorting, applying rules — and completely hopeless at what you do without thinking: recognizing a face, understanding a
sentence, catching a ball. A machine could beat you at chess decades before it could tell a cat from a dog. Modern
artificial intelligence grew out of that paradox, and out of one idea that still feels a bit like cheating: instead of
writing the rules, make the machine learn them.

## 1. What artificial intelligence is

"Artificial intelligence" is a marketing term as much as a technical one, so let's use a working definition: a system
that performs tasks we'd normally say require intelligence — recognizing, predicting, deciding, generating. People
usually split it into three levels:

- **Narrow AI**: good at one task. A chess engine, a spam filter, an object detector, a chatbot. Everything that exists
today is here, including the most impressive systems you've used.
- **General AI (AGI)**: as capable as a human across the board, able to learn a new task the way you would. Doesn't
exist. Whether we're close is one of the loudest debates in tech.
- **Superintelligence**: beyond human capability across the board. Science fiction for now, taken seriously as a
long-term question by serious people.

The examples worth naming change every year, which tells you something about the field's pace.

## 2. Programming rules versus learning from data

An ordinary program is a list of rules written by a human: *if* the sensor reads this, *then* do that. It works
beautifully as long as someone can actually write the rules. Now try writing the rule for "this photo contains a cat."
Whiskers? Some dogs have whiskers. Pointy ears? Not from behind. Fur texture? Not in low light. Nobody has ever written
that rule, and nobody ever will.

Machine learning flips the process. We give the machine examples and the expected answers, and *it* builds the rule — as
a function with thousands or millions of parameters, adjusted automatically until its answers match the examples. The
programmer no longer writes the solution. They write the way of learning it, which is a very different job.

## 3. An example: recognizing a 9

Let's make this concrete with the "hello world" of machine learning: MNIST, a dataset of 70,000 handwritten digits, each
a $28 \times 28$ grayscale image. Your job: given the image, say which digit it is.

Try the rules approach first. A 9 is a circle with a stick, right? Write a circle detector, write a vertical-line
detector, and if both fire, it's a 9. This actually sort of works — until someone writes a 9 slightly tilted. Or with an
open loop. Or shifted to the left. Or with the tail curved. Each variation breaks a rule, so you patch it with another
rule, and another, and the pile of patches never ends, because the ways people write a 9 never end.

These variations have a name: *factors of variation* — position, slant, thickness, style, lighting. The lesson
generalizes brutally: for real images (a game piece anywhere on a field, under arena lighting), hand-written rules don't
just struggle, they're hopeless.

The way out is called *representation learning*: don't just learn the final answer — learn the features themselves. Let
the machine discover, from the data, which strokes and shapes matter for telling a 9 from a 4. That idea, pushed hard,
is deep learning, and it's the subject of the [next lesson](/blog/ai-neural-networks).

## 4. What "learning" means

Can we say precisely what it means for a program to learn? Tom Mitchell's classic definition: a program learns from
experience $E$ with respect to a task $T$ and a performance measure $P$ if its performance on $T$, as measured by $P$,
improves with $E$.

Sounds bureaucratic, but it's actually sharp. Three ingredients, always:

- a **task** $T$ — recognize the digit, detect the game piece;
- a **performance measure** $P$ — the percentage classified correctly;
- an **experience** $E$ — thousands of labeled images.

Learning is simply: $P$ goes up as $E$ accumulates. Note the subtlety — the task is not "to learn". If we want a robot
to detect game pieces, $T$ is detecting game pieces; learning is what happens on the way there. In FRC terms: $T$ =
put a box around every game piece in the camera image, $E$ = a folder of labeled photos from past events, $P$ = how
often the boxes are right on photos it has never seen.

## 5. The kinds of learning

Three big families, split by what kind of feedback the machine gets.

**Supervised learning** — we provide the answers. Show the algorithm images of bananas labeled "banana" and pears
labeled "pear", and let it adjust itself until it stops mixing them up. Most of what touches our world (image
classification, object detection) is supervised. The bottleneck is exactly what it sounds like: someone has to label
all that data.

**Unsupervised learning** — data, but no answers. The algorithm's job is to find structure on its own: group similar
customers, compress images, spot the weird data point. Nobody tells it what the groups mean.

**Reinforcement learning** — no labels at all, just rewards. The system acts, the environment scores it, and behaviors
that lead to reward get reinforced. This is how AlphaZero learned chess and Go: it played millions of games against
itself, starting from random moves, with "did I win?" as the only teacher — and ended up beating world champions. It's
also a big part of how robots learn to walk in simulation. If any of this course makes you want to go down a rabbit
hole, make it this one.

## 6. Data, training, testing

One habit separates people who do ML from people who get fooled by it: never grade a model on data it trained on.

An example, for a machine, is a vector of features — for an image, literally the pixel values. The dataset gets split:
a **training set** the model learns from, and a **test set** it never sees until the end. Why so strict? Because a model
with millions of parameters can *memorize* its training data — perfect scores in practice, useless on anything new.
That failure is called *overfitting*, and it's the default outcome, not the exception. Generalizing — being right on
examples you've never seen — is the entire game. (In between, a *validation set* is used to tune settings without
contaminating the final exam.)

Keep that lens for the rest of the chapter: whenever someone claims a model "works", your first question is now
"on data it trained on, or on data it had never seen?"

## What about the robot?

So where does any of this show up on our robot? Almost nowhere — and that's worth saying clearly. AprilTag detection
and all of the localization course use zero machine learning: the detector is hand-written rules, and they work
precisely because the tags were designed to make the rules easy. The one exception: a Limelight can run a *neural
detector* to find game pieces, which is supervised learning end to end — labeled photos in, boxes out. That's the
subject of [Object detection](/blog/ai-object-detection).
