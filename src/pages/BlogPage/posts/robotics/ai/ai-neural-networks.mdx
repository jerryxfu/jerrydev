I'll ruin the mystery in the first paragraph: a neural network is a function. Numbers go in, numbers come out, and in
between there is nothing but multiplications, additions, and a few simple functions applied millions of times. No
thoughts, no neurons in any biological sense. What makes it remarkable (and it *is* remarkable) is that its parameters
are not written by anyone. They are adjusted automatically until the function does what we want. This lesson is about
that adjustment.

## 1. A network is a function

Think of a function as an input → output machine: $x \mapsto f(x)$. A neural network is a function $f_\theta(x)$ whose
*shape* is fixed by us and whose parameters $\theta$ (the millions of little numbers inside) are free. Learning is
nothing more than choosing $\theta$.

What can $x$ and $f_\theta(x)$ be? Almost anything you can turn into numbers. The 784 pixels of an MNIST image in, ten
digit scores out. A camera frame in, boxes around game pieces out. A sentence in, the next word out. It's the same recipe
every time. Only the size changes.

---

## 2. The neuron

The building block is almost insultingly simple. A neuron takes inputs $x_1, \dots, x_n$, forms a weighted sum with
weights $w_1, \dots, w_n$, adds a bias $b$, then applies an activation function $\sigma$:

$$
y = \sigma\!\left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i + b \right) = \sigma\!\left( w^\top x + b \right)
$$

The weights say how much each input matters (a negative weight means "this input argues against me"), the bias shifts
the threshold, and the activation (ReLU, sigmoid, tanh) bends the output. That bend matters more than it looks. Without
it, stacking neurons would be pointless, because a weighted sum of weighted sums is still just a weighted sum. The
non-linearity is what lets depth buy you anything.

Let's do one by hand, with two inputs. Say $w = (2, -1)$, $b = -1$, and the activation is ReLU, which just means "keep
positive values, replace negative ones with 0". For the input $x = (1, 1)$, the weighted sum is
$2 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 1 - 1 = 0$, so the neuron outputs 0. For $x = (2, 0)$, the sum is $2 \cdot 2 - 0 - 1 = 3$, so it
outputs 3. That's it. That's a neuron. A network is millions of these, wired together.

## 3. Layers

A network stacks neurons in layers: each neuron of a layer reads every output of the previous one. For a whole layer at
once, the weights form a matrix $W$ and the computation collapses into one line:

$$
h = \sigma(W x + b)
$$

One matrix-vector product per layer. That's the whole forward pass. Do this a few dozen times and you have the biggest
networks in the world. It's also why graphics cards, machines built to multiply matrices very fast, are the hardware of
this entire field. Deep learning became practical the day someone noticed gamers had already paid for the right chips.

Let's count the parameters of a small MNIST network, because the number surprises people. The input has 784 pixels. Say
the first layer has 128 neurons: that's $784 \times 128 = 100\,352$ weights, plus 128 biases. The output layer has 10
neurons (one per digit) reading those 128 values: $128 \times 10 = 1\,280$ weights, plus 10 biases. In total, about
101,770 parameters, for a network that fits in fifteen lines of code and reads handwritten digits with over 97%
accuracy.

## 4. Learning is minimizing an error

So how do you choose a hundred thousand parameters, or a hundred billion? Not by hand. You define a **loss function**: a
single number measuring how wrong the network currently is (for example, the squared gap between its outputs and the
right answers, averaged over examples). Now learning has become an optimization problem: find the $\theta$ that makes
the loss small.

The algorithm is **gradient descent**, and the picture to keep is a ball rolling downhill. The loss is a landscape over
parameter space. The gradient says which direction is uphill. So you take a small step the *other* way, and repeat, a few
million times:

$$
\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta)
$$

The step size $\eta$ is the *learning rate*, and it's the first knob everyone learns to respect. Too small, and training
takes forever. Too large, and the ball ricochets across the valley without ever settling. There's no grand theory telling
you the perfect value. People try, watch the loss curve, and adjust. Welcome to the empirical side of the field.

## 5. Backpropagation in one picture

One piece is missing: the gradient. The loss depends on millions of parameters through dozens of stacked layers, so how
do you get the slope with respect to *each one*? The answer is **backpropagation**: the error at the output is passed
backwards through the network, layer by layer, and each layer computes how much it contributed. Underneath, it's nothing
deeper than the chain rule from calculus, applied very systematically.

Two things to retain, and one thing to skip. Retain that it's mechanical: no insight happens inside backprop, just
bookkeeping. And retain that it's cheap: one backward pass costs about as much as one forward pass. Skip deriving it by
hand. PyTorch and friends do it automatically for any function you write, which is honestly one of the quiet miracles of
modern software.

---

## 6. The training loop

Put the pieces together and you get a loop so short it fits on a slide: grab a small **batch** of examples, run the
forward pass, measure the loss, backpropagate, take one gradient step. Repeat until you've been through the whole dataset
(that's one **epoch**), then go again, watching the loss curve fall. When the loss on *held-out* data stops improving,
stop. Past that point you're memorizing, not learning ([remember overfitting?](/blog/ai-basics)).

```python
# The whole idea, in PyTorch. Missing only the data loading.
import torch
from torch import nn

model = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Flatten(),
    nn.Linear(28 * 28, 128),
    nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Linear(128, 10),
)
loss_fn = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()
optimizer = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.1)

for images, labels in train_loader:      # one batch at a time
    loss = loss_fn(model(images), labels)  # how wrong are we?
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()                        # backpropagation
    optimizer.step()                       # one step downhill
```

Those fifteen or so lines, scaled up and refined, are the same skeleton behind every model you've heard of. That's not an
exaggeration. It's the field's best-kept open secret.
