## What got decided since devlog #0

A few things got settled in the two weeks since the plan. All of them before the hybrid model exists, and that's on purpose.

- **M4, attention fusion, is dropped.** One way of fusing the two vectors (concatenation) is enough for one essay. The attention version goes back on the shelf for a later personal project.
- **Two training goals, reported separately.** A model can learn to put the one true disease on top, or to reproduce the whole reference differential. The second is called soft-target, and every hybrid result will be reported under both.
- **DDR@10 joins the scores** as a secondary metric, because the reference differential has about nine entries and five slots can't hold it.
- **Two comparisons are locked:** hybrid vs text-only, and hybrid vs TF-IDF, each on clean text, lay language and typos. Locking them now stops me from picking whichever comparison happens to look best later.
- **Everything runs on the MacBook Air.** The desktop GPU is reserved for the optional fine-tuning run, if it ever happens.
- **SapBERT now reads the whole note.** The plan had set a limit of 128 word pieces per note, before any note existed. Measured after the fact, that cut the end off most notes. The limit is now 512, which covers everything, and the text-only model was re-run. The story of how I found out is below.

## The shape of the experiment, in three paragraphs

**The models.** Seven ways to turn a patient note into a ranked list of 49 diseases. Three baselines: B0 matches keywords from a fixed list, B1 counts words (TF-IDF), and B2 is an oracle that reads the true symptom codes and never the text, so it marks the top of the scale. Then the learned ones: M1 is a frozen biomedical language model, SapBERT, with a small classifier on top. M2 will read only the medical concept codes extracted from the text. M3, the one the whole question is about, glues M1's vector and M2's vector end to end. [Details and the reasoning behind each →](/blog/medive-reference#the-models)

**The four versions of the text.** The test patients never change, only the words do. Tier 0 is the clean templated note. Tier 1 swaps symptom names for medical synonyms (optional). Tier 2 swaps them for everyday wording, the way a patient would actually say it. Tier 3 adds typos. Models train on tier 0 only, and tier 2 is the one that carries the argument. [The tiers →](/blog/medive-reference#the-four-tiers)

**The scores.** Against the true disease: Acc@k (is it in the top k?), MRR (how high up is it?), macro-F1 (rare diseases count as much as common ones). Against the reference differential: DDR@5, how much of the reference list the top five recovers, and DDP@5, how much of the top five is justified. DDR@5 has a ceiling of 65.7 percent on this test set, because a list of five can't hold nine diseases. Every condition runs on five seeds, and every table is mean ± standard deviation. [Formulas, the ceiling, a worked example →](/blog/medive-reference#the-scores)

Three things fall out of this table and the typo tier.

### 1. Clean text is too easy to tell the models apart

The oracle scores 99.7 at rank 1 and the cheap baselines are within a point of it. Every model puts the true disease in its top three every single time. Since even the oracle, which sees the true evidences, sits at the ceiling, the ceiling belongs to the task and the dataset, not to my rendering. The first sub-question, relevance on clean text, is going to read as a tie. The plan said so in advance, so at least that's not a surprise.

### 2. The differential list is where the room is

Every standard model recovers somewhere between a third and forty percent of the reference list, against a ceiling of 65.7. The soft-target run shows why. Trained on the full list instead of the single true disease, the *same features* reach 59.3 -- at the cost of Acc@1 dropping to 75.8. So the quality of the list follows what we ask the model to learn, not what it reads. That's why the hybrid will be tested under both training goals.

### 3. The language model is still the one that minds typos, just a lot less than I first thought

The plan expected the keyword matcher to suffer most from noise. The reverse happened, and on the first run it looked dramatic.

| Condition | Acc@1, clean | Acc@1, typos | DDR@5, clean | DDR@5, typos |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| B0 keyword match + LR | 98.8 | 98.7 | 40.1 | 39.0 |
| B1 TF-IDF + LR | 99.3 | 98.8 | 39.5 | 34.7 |
| M1 SapBERT text-only | 98.9 | 95.0 | 34.4 | 32.1 |
| M1 soft-target | 75.8 | 51.0 | 59.3 | 54.5 |

<img className="diagram" src={_figTypoDrop} alt="Acc@1 and DDR@5 per condition, clean versus typos, with the SapBERT conditions dropping the most" />

Word counting still sees the other hundred words in the record. Fuzzy keyword matching forgives spelling by construction. SapBERT does neither, and loses about four points at rank 1 where the baselines lose half a point or nothing. That's the finding as it stands now. The first version of this table, from a run a few days earlier, had M1 at 83.6 under typos -- a fifteen-point drop. Here's what happened to the other eleven points.

### Looking inside the language model

BERT-family models don't read words. They read *word pieces*. A tokeniser called WordPiece carries a fixed vocabulary of about thirty thousand pieces, and any word that isn't in it gets split into the longest pieces it does know. A clean medical word is usually one piece. A misspelled one isn't. Here's SapBERT's own tokeniser on words from patient `test_12`, clean on the left, tier 3 on the right:

| Clean | Pieces | Typo | Pieces |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| significantly | `significantly` | signifcantly | `sign` `##if` `##can` `##tl` `##y` |
| cough | `cough` | couhg | `cou` `##hg` |
| symptoms | `symptoms` | sypmtoms | `sy` `##pm` `##to` `##ms` |
| heavy | `heavy` | hevay | `hev` `##ay` |

The `##` marks a piece that continues the previous one. Those fragments were never learned as "significantly" or "cough". M1 averages every piece's vector into one vector per patient, so every corrupted word pulls the average a bit off course. That was my whole explanation, and it's true as far as it goes.

Then I counted the pieces. The fragments make the note longer: {stats.wordpiece.pieces_mean_clean.toFixed(0)} pieces on average at tier 0, {stats.wordpiece.pieces_mean_typos.toFixed(0)} at tier 3. And the plan had told SapBERT to read only the first 128. That cut landed on {stats.wordpiece.share_over_128_clean.toFixed(0)} percent of clean notes and on {stats.wordpiece.share_over_128_typos.toFixed(0)} percent of typo notes. The notes are written symptoms first, history last, so what fell off the end was the patient's history: smoking, asthma, travel, contact with sick people. And typos, by making the note longer, pushed even more of it past the edge.

<Callout kind="important">
    The limit of 128 was a guess written into the plan before a single note had been rendered, with a note to "justify from the 95th percentile" that nobody went back to. The 95th percentile turned out to be 264. Raising the limit to 512, so that every note is read whole, moved M1 under typos from 83.6 to 95.0 at rank 1. Most of the "collapse" was the cut, not the tokeniser. The tokeniser accounts for the four points that remain.
</Callout>

That's exactly the kind of mistake the methodology section exists to catch, and it's going in there as a corrected assumption rather than being quietly fixed. It also changes what tier 3 measures: it's now a test of the tokeniser and the pooling, as intended, instead of a test of which sentences happened to get cut.

This one is more interesting than I expected. A note at tier 3 has about {stats.corrupted_words_mean.toFixed(0)} corrupted words on average. For B1 (TF-IDF), accuracy is flat no matter how many words are broken. For M1 it dips to about {stats.acc_by_corrupted_words[5].m1_acc1.toFixed(0)} percent in the middle bins and then *recovers* on the notes with the most corrupted words. That's not typos helping. The notes with the most corrupted words are the longest notes, the ones with the most evidence, and now that the model reads all of it, they're the easy ones. On the first run, before the limit was raised, this curve fell steadily to 74 percent and never came back -- because those long notes were the ones being cut the most.

<Callout kind="note">
    One caveat. The renderer draws sentence order and template variants from a generator seeded per tier, so the tier-3 note isn't exactly the tier-0 note with typos added: sentences also come in a different order, and some use a different wording template. Bag-of-words models don't notice. A transformer might, a little. So a small part of M1's tier-3 drop may be re-rendering rather than typos. A control run would settle it, and it's on the list.
</Callout>

## Poking around SapBERT's space

Since this post is the fun one, a few more pictures. None of them are going in the essay as they stand, but they taught me something about the model I'm building on.

### The 49 diseases, two ways

SapBERT has never been told what the 49 pathologies are. But it can still place them, in two different ways. On the left, each pathology is the average vector of its test patients' notes. On the right, I fed SapBERT the 49 pathology *names* and nothing else, which is closer to what it was actually trained for. Both are squashed to two dimensions with PCA, which keeps distances honest for a small set of points.

<img className="diagram" src={_figDiseaseMaps} alt="Two scatter plots of the 49 pathologies: one from the mean note vector per pathology, one from the embedded pathology names" />

The two views disagree more than I expected. On average only {(space.views_agree.mean_neighbour_overlap * 100).toFixed(0)} percent of a pathology's five nearest neighbours are the same in both. The note view groups diseases by what patients say: the whole respiratory-infection family piles up in one spot, the cardiac chest-pain family in another. The name view groups them by how they're spelled: "stable angina" next to "unstable angina", "bronchitis" next to "bronchiolitis", the two rhinosinusitis next to each other. The first is what the classifier gets to use. The second is the ontology's view sneaking in through the words themselves.

### Are confusions between neighbours?

Same 49 centroids, as a similarity matrix, sorted so that similar pathologies sit together. The red circles are M1's most frequent rank-1 mistakes on the typo tier, drawn at the row of the true pathology and the column of what M1 answered.

<img className="diagram" src={_figDiseaseHeatmap} alt="Clustered heatmap of cosine similarity between the 49 pathology centroids, with M1's top confusions circled" />

The bright squares are the rhinosinusitis pair and the angina family, and that's where the circles land. Across all of M1's rank-1 errors on this tier, {space.confusions.share_to_top5_neighbour.toFixed(0)} percent go to one of the true pathology's five nearest centroids. So when the model is wrong, it's mostly wrong in the direction the space already leans. Whether those neighbours are clinically adjacent is a question for the error analysis, and that part is mine to write.

### The lay phrases, before any model runs on them

The tier 2 hypothesis is that SapBERT can bridge "the runs" to "diarrhea" without being told. That can be checked on the phrases alone, before a single model is trained on tier 2. Blue dots are the {space.phrases.n_canonical} canonical symptom phrases the templates use; green dots are the {space.phrases.n_lay} lay paraphrases written for them; a line joins each paraphrase to the phrase it replaces.

<img className="diagram" src={_figPhraseMap} alt="UMAP of canonical symptom phrases and their lay paraphrases, each pair joined by a line" />

Short lines are the easy cases: "coughing up a bit of blood" lands right on top of "coughing up blood". Long lines are the ones the language model doesn't get for free. If I ask, for each lay phrase, whether the *closest* canonical phrase in SapBERT's space is the right one, the answer is yes for {space.phrases.lay_nn_accuracy.toFixed(0)} percent of them. The ones it misses by the widest margin are idioms, phrases that share no word at all with the medical wording.

The surprising row: doing the same test with the canonical phrases *typo'd* instead of paraphrased, SapBERT finds the right phrase {space.phrases.typo_nn_accuracy.toFixed(0)} percent of the time. At the level of a single phrase, typos are less of a problem than lay wording. That fits the corrected tier-3 result above: once the whole note is read, typos cost a few points, not fifteen.

### One word, one edit at a time

The cleanest version of the WordPiece story. Take a symptom word, apply one random character edit, then another, and measure how far its vector moves from the clean word, averaged over {space.word_typos.trials} random edit paths.

<img className="diagram" src={_figWordTypos} alt="Left: cosine distance of a word from its clean form against the number of character edits. Right: number of word pieces the tokeniser produces" />

The first edit does most of the damage: a mean distance of {space.word_typos.distance_after_1_edit_mean.toFixed(2)} after one edit, {space.word_typos.distance_after_3_edits_mean.toFixed(2)} after three. The right panel is why. A clean word is one piece. One edit turns it into two, three or four pieces that have nothing to do with the original. After that, more edits barely matter -- the word is already gone. Compare that to the note-level drift of about {stats.drift_mean.toFixed(2)}: a single word gets wrecked, but a note has a hundred other words to average it against.
